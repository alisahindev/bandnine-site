IELTS pronunciation: what the examiner listens for

In short

  • Clear speech matters more than adopting a particular accent.
  • Practise stress and pausing in complete answers, not only isolated words.
  • Ask a listener which word they missed before choosing a drill.
  • Check a new answer to see whether a practised feature transfers.

IELTS pronunciation is about making your speech understandable and using your voice to carry meaning. You do not need to replace your accent. You do need to notice when a listener misses a word, loses the end of a sentence or cannot hear the difference between two ideas. A useful practice session starts with one of those problems and checks whether your next attempt is clearer.

What the examiner listens for

Pronunciation is one of the four Speaking criteria. The examiner listens throughout the test, including your short answers in Part 1 and your longer explanations in Part 3. Individual sounds matter, but so do word stress, the emphasis within a sentence and the way you divide speech into meaningful groups.

The IELTS website explains the difference between accent and intelligibility. The practical question is how much effort your listener needs to understand you. A recognisable accent can coexist with clear speech. Copying somebody else's accent will not solve a habit of swallowing the important word at the end of each sentence.

Use the guide to the Speaking band descriptors to see where Pronunciation fits alongside the other criteria. A written answer cannot show this criterion: you need to listen to the recording.

Find the word a listener actually misses

Record a short answer about a familiar activity. Ask a practice partner to listen without seeing a transcript and write down the main idea. Then ask them to identify any word they could not catch. This gives you a smaller, more useful target than asking whether your pronunciation sounds good.

Suppose you say, I usually walk to the market, but yesterday I worked at home. If your listener hears the same verb twice, practise the difference in the phrases, not only the words on their own. Check a dictionary recording of each word, listen for the vowel and the ending, then record the complete sentence again.

Choose words you actually need. Spending a whole session on a difficult sound that never appears in your answers can leave a more frequent problem untouched. Keep a short list of misunderstood words and cross one out only when a listener understands it in a new sentence.

Put stress in the right part of a word

In a word with several syllables, one syllable usually carries the main stress. Giving every syllable equal weight can make a familiar word harder to recognise. Looking at the spelling alone will not always tell you where the stress belongs.

Take a word from yesterday's answer, such as photography. Listen to its dictionary audio and notice that the main stress falls on the second syllable. Compare it with photograph, where the first syllable carries the main stress. Say each word in a short phrase, then use it in a personal answer.

I started photography last winter because I wanted to take better pictures of the coast.

Do not turn your practice list into a vocabulary collection. Four words that you can use clearly in connected speech are enough for one session. Revisit them several days later without reading the stress marks.

Emphasise the information that changes the meaning

Sentence stress helps a listener hear what matters. Consider the sentence I borrowed the camera from my brother. Emphasising borrowed tells us that you did not buy it. Emphasising brother tells us who lent it to you. The grammar stays the same, but the message changes.

Try giving the sentence in response to two different questions: did you buy the camera, and who lent it to you? Your emphasis should follow the answer the listener needs. There is no need to make every content word louder.

In a longer response, choose the contrast before speaking. You might say, The room was small, but the windows made it feel open. Let the contrast between small and open be audible. Stress works best when you know what you want the listener to notice.

Pause between ideas rather than inside phrases

A pause can make a long answer easier to follow. The useful place is usually a boundary in the meaning, such as after a reason or before a new example. Repeated pauses between an article and its noun can make the listener reconstruct the phrase.

Write a sentence from your recording and mark possible boundaries with a slash: When I moved into the flat / I knew nobody nearby / so I started going to a local walking group. The slashes are practice marks, not an instruction to stop at every possible boundary in the test.

Say the thought groups separately, then join them at a comfortable pace. Keep related words together. If you run out of breath, shorten the sentence rather than pushing through the final words so quickly that they disappear.

Use intonation to make the connection audible

Intonation is the movement of your pitch. It helps show that an idea continues, that you are making a contrast or that you have finished. You do not need a tune for every sentence, and speaking with exaggerated pitch changes can distract from the meaning.

Try a short list: We brought sandwiches, some fruit and a flask of tea. Keep the earlier items sounding connected, then let the final item sound complete. Next try a contrast: I expected a crowded room, but there were only a few people there. Listen for whether the second part sounds like a change in your expectation.

These are practice choices rather than fixed rules for every speaker. Judge them by whether the meaning becomes easier to follow.

A daily 10-minute drill

Use a recording you can replay, one clear model for the feature you are practising and a fresh question. Work through this sequence:

  1. Spend 2 minutes listening to a short answer you recorded. Choose one unclear word or one sentence whose meaning is hard to follow.
  2. Spend 2 minutes checking a dictionary recording or a short spoken model. Listen for the particular feature you selected.
  3. Spend 3 minutes saying the phrase and then the whole sentence. Record one attempt without speaking over the model.
  4. Spend 3 minutes answering a different question that lets you use the same feature. Listen without reading your notes.

The last step matters. Repeating a model shows that you can imitate it; using the feature while choosing your own words shows whether it is becoming available in conversation.

Review the sound, not only the transcript

A transcript can help you locate a sentence, but it cannot show whether its stress or rhythm made the meaning clear. Speech recognition may also guess a word from context. A correct transcription is therefore not enough evidence that the word was clear.

Compare two short recordings with the same listening question: can someone hear the key word on the first attempt? Keep the comparison narrow. Once that feature is more dependable, move to another and return to the first in a later answer.

Questions people ask

Do I need a British accent for IELTS Speaking?

No. Any accent is acceptable when your speech is understandable. Focus on the sounds, stress and rhythm that help a listener follow your meaning.

How can I practise IELTS pronunciation every day?

Use a 10-minute routine: identify one unclear phrase, listen to a model, record the phrase in a sentence, then answer a new question using the same feature.

Can I check my pronunciation from a transcript?

A transcript can locate words but cannot show how stress, pauses or intonation carry meaning. Listen to the recording and, when possible, ask someone to identify words they could not hear clearly.
