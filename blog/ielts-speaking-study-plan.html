IELTS Speaking preparation: a four-week study plan

In short

  • Start with an uninterrupted mock test and record specific difficulties.
  • Give each week one main criterion while continuing to answer complete questions.
  • Use daily 20-minute sessions for a drill, fresh speaking and review.
  • Compare different questions to check whether practice carries into unfamiliar answers.
  • Keep the final three days focused on familiar routines.

IELTS Speaking preparation needs a routine that turns one recorded difficulty into something you can practise the next day. A calendar full of mock tests is not enough if you keep making the same mistake without working on it. This four-week plan uses a daily 20-minute session, with one main criterion each week and a few full mock tests to check what carries into unfamiliar questions.

Four weeks is a planning period, not a promise about your eventual band. Keep the routine if it helps you study consistently, and change the focus when the evidence in your recordings points elsewhere.

Start with one uninterrupted mock test

On the first day, record a full Speaking practice test. Use unfamiliar questions and follow the three-part order. Give yourself 1 minute to prepare for Part 2 and up to 2 minutes for the long turn. Avoid restarting after a mistake: you need to hear how you manage it while continuing.

The IELTS website provides Speaking sample tasks and confirms that the Speaking test is the same for Academic and General Training. Use the format to organise your baseline recording, rather than reading sample answers aloud.

Afterwards, find two moments that were difficult to follow. Write the time in the recording, your actual words and what went wrong. Add one next action. For example, a repeated pause before past-tense verbs suggests a short practice activity with past events, rather than another general conversation about your hobbies.

You can use the guide to the Speaking band descriptors to connect that evidence with the four criteria. Do not assign yourself a band from one sentence. If you cannot tell what caused a problem, ask a teacher or practice partner to listen to that particular passage.

Keep a repeatable 20-minute session

Use the same structure on ordinary practice days so that planning the session does not consume it:

  1. Listen for 3 minutes. Replay yesterday's target passage and decide what you want to change today.
  2. Drill for 4 minutes. Work on the phrase, structure or speech habit behind that problem.
  3. Speak for 5 minutes. Answer fresh questions or give a long turn, using the feature without reading a prepared answer.
  4. Review and retry for 5 minutes. Listen to one part of the recording, then say a clearer version aloud.
  5. Log for 3 minutes. Note what changed, what still needs attention and the question you will use next time.

On a full mock day, replace this sequence with the test and a short review. An 11–14-minute test leaves 6–9 minutes within your session for identifying the next focus. Do not fit a second full routine into the same slot.

Week one focuses on Fluency and coherence

Start by making your answers easier to follow. In Part 1, answer the question before adding background. In Part 3, explain one reason far enough that the listener understands it. In Part 2, notice where your story loses its direction rather than counting every hesitation.

Use a question such as why people return to the same holiday destination. Record one answer, then describe its main point in a single sentence. If you cannot do that, your next attempt needs a clearer direction.

Familiar places take less planning. If a family already knows where to stay, they can spend their short holiday resting instead of working out how everything works.

The example develops one reason. During the week, practise the same kind of development with new subjects. Keep normal pauses; removing every pause is not the goal.

Week two focuses on Lexical resource

Take vocabulary from gaps in your own answers. If you repeatedly call places nice, decide what you meant: quiet enough to read, convenient for buses or well maintained. Practise the phrase that expresses that meaning, with words that commonly occur together.

Keep a small list of useful combinations, each attached to an answer you have actually given. Say The library is within walking distance, so I go there after work. Then use within walking distance in an answer about shopping. Changing the context checks whether you understand the phrase beyond one sentence.

Include paraphrasing. Explain an object without naming it, then continue the answer. At the end of day 14, take another full mock with different questions. Listen for whether you can find another expression when the exact word does not arrive.

Week three focuses on Grammatical range and accuracy

Choose a recurring pattern, not every error in the transcript. If past events keep slipping into the present, spend your drill time contrasting yesterday with your usual routine. If your sentences stop after because, practise finishing the reason before adding another idea.

I normally cook at home, but yesterday I ate out because I had stayed late at work.

Here, the tense changes follow changes in time. Say your own version about a different event. A sentence you understand is more useful than a complicated structure you cannot adapt.

Later in the week, answer a Part 3 question that needs a condition, such as what might persuade people to use buses more often. Check whether the condition and its consequence belong together. Continue practising longer answers so that grammar work remains connected to speaking.

Week four focuses on Pronunciation

Listen to the audio before looking at the transcript. Choose a word a listener could not catch, or a sentence whose emphasis hid your main point. Practise it in a short phrase and then in an answer.

For example, I wanted to go on Saturday, but we went on Sunday. The contrast between the days needs to be audible. Raising your volume across the whole sentence does not help the listener identify that contrast.

Keep earlier targets active by including them in your new answers. You are changing the main focus, not abandoning the other criteria. If the baseline showed that pronunciation was your clearest difficulty, move this week earlier and keep one main criterion per week.

Use mock tests to check transfer

Compare the first recording with day 14 and another full mock on day 25. Use different questions but similar recording conditions. A polished repeat of the same answer can show useful rehearsal without showing how you cope with something unfamiliar.

For each comparison, look at the problem you originally logged. Is the past time clearer? Does the explanation stay connected? Can the listener catch the important word without replaying it? Keep the actual passage as evidence rather than relying on how confident you felt.

If the problem remains, make the drill narrower. Work on one tense contrast or one sentence boundary. If it appears less often across several new answers, choose another target. These checks guide study decisions; they do not turn a home recording into an examiner's assessment.

Keep the last three days familiar

On day 26, revisit the error log and practise two recurring difficulties. On day 27, do a short selection of familiar question types and check your test appointment instructions. On day 28, keep speaking practice light and review the routine for entering each part.

These days are for maintaining habits you already use. Avoid learning a long answer that you would have to force onto an unexpected question. If you miss a session earlier in the month, resume with the next small task; doubling the following session is not required.

Questions people ask

Is four weeks enough for IELTS Speaking preparation?

That depends on your starting point and what you need to change. Four weeks gives you a manageable practice cycle, not a promised band. Compare recordings on unfamiliar questions and adjust the plan when recurring difficulties remain.

How long should I practise IELTS Speaking each day?

This plan uses 20 minutes a day, including listening, a focused drill, speaking and review. On mock days, replace that sequence with the full test and a short review rather than adding another session.

How often should I take a full Speaking mock test?

In this plan, take one at the start, one on day 14 and one on day 25. Use different questions each time. Spend the intervening days practising difficulties from the recordings.
