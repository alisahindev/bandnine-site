IELTS Speaking Part 1: question types and how to answer

21 October 2026 · 6 min read · How we write these guides

In short

  • Give a direct answer and add a relevant detail when it helps explain your meaning.
  • There is no fixed sentence count or duration for every Part 1 answer.
  • Listen for changes in time before choosing your verbs.
  • Use original practice questions to develop flexible answers rather than memorised speeches.

IELTS Speaking Part 1 asks you about familiar aspects of your life. The vocabulary may be everyday English, but the questions still need attention. You might be asked about a present habit, a childhood memory or something you would like to change. A useful answer responds to that particular question and adds enough detail to make your meaning clear.

The examples below are invented candidate answers. Use them to notice how an answer develops, then choose details from your own life. Learning someone else's sentences makes it harder to respond when the wording changes.

Know what familiar topics ask you to do

Home, work and study are useful starting points for practice. You should also be able to discuss everyday preferences, such as what you cook or how you spend a quiet evening. Build small groups of relevant words around these subjects, rather than writing a complete speech for each one.

For home, you could collect language for the type of building and its location. Then add phrases you would use to explain a preference: within walking distance or too noisy in the evening. Use only phrases whose meaning you understand.

The IELTS website's guide to the three parts describes Part 1 as a conversation about familiar topics. Familiar does not mean identical: listen to the wording before deciding which detail to give.

Let the question set the answer length

There is no fixed number of sentences or seconds you must use for every Part 1 answer. A short factual question may need less explanation than a question about why your preferences have changed. Give the answer first, add a useful detail and notice whether you have reached a natural stopping point.

Compare Yes, I do with Yes, I often cook in the evening. I spend most of the day at a desk, so preparing dinner gives me something practical to do. The second response gives the examiner more language to hear and explains the answer.

You do not need a further story about every meal you have made. If the next sentence repeats your reason in different words, stop. The examiner can ask another question when more is needed.

Use an answer, a reason and a detail when they fit

This pattern helps when a question asks about a preference. First state your preference. Explain why it matters, then add a detail if the reason still feels vague. It is a thinking aid, not a script that every answer must follow.

For a question about reading at home, a response could be: I prefer reading at the kitchen table because the light is better there. My bedroom only has a small lamp, and I find long articles tiring to read in it. The detail makes the reason concrete.

A factual question may need a different shape. I work in a small shop that sells household items. Most of my day involves helping customers find what they need. That answer explains the job. Adding an unrelated opinion would make it less focused.

Notice the time before choosing a tense

Topic practice often goes wrong when you rehearse only the present. Listen for words such as usually, when you were younger and would like. They change the job your verbs need to do.

  • A present habit: I usually walk to work because the route through the park is pleasant.
  • A finished period: When I was younger, I travelled to school by bus.
  • A change continuing into the present: I've started cycling more often since I moved.
  • A possible future preference: I'd like to live closer to my family, although I would miss this area.

Practise these changes around one subject before adding another. Ask yourself about your home now, your childhood home and the home you would like later. The aim is to connect time and meaning, not to insert a different tense into every sentence.

Answer honestly when a topic does not fit

You may dislike an activity or have little experience of it. Say that and offer the closest relevant explanation. You do not have to invent an enthusiastic hobby to keep speaking.

If asked about gardening, for example: I don't do much gardening because I live in a flat without a balcony. I do keep a small plant on the kitchen windowsill, although I sometimes forget to water it. The answer stays on the subject while acknowledging limited experience.

If you are neither working nor studying, describe your current situation briefly. You might be looking for work or taking time to decide what to study. Practise the language you actually need to explain your circumstances, instead of borrowing a model answer about an imaginary degree.

Ten original practice questions

These questions were written for practice. Use them to exercise different response types. Start without notes, then listen to your recording before trying an answer again.

  1. Which room in your home do you use most during the day?
  2. What would you like to change about the area where you live?
  3. What did you enjoy doing near your home when you were a child?
  4. Which part of your working or studying day requires the most concentration?
  5. Has the way you organise your day changed recently?
  6. Do you prefer spending a quiet evening alone or with someone else?
  7. When did you last learn to cook something new?
  8. How often do you use a library?
  9. Is there a practical skill you would like to learn?
  10. Do you usually make plans for the weekend in advance?

On a second attempt, keep your main answer but change one supporting detail. This checks whether you can express the idea in more than one way. Do not keep repeating until you can recite the first version word for word.

Listen for evidence in your recording

Pick two answers and write down exactly what you said. Check whether the first sentence answers the question. Then circle repeated words and mark any place where you abandoned a sentence. Choose one problem that you can hear, rather than deciding the whole recording is poor.

The examiner listens for Fluency and coherence, Lexical resource, Grammatical range and accuracy, and Pronunciation across the Speaking test. The guide to the public band descriptors explains those criteria. For a short practice session, turn one observation into one action: finish the sentence before correcting it, for example, or replace an unclear word with a phrase you know.

Practise handing the conversation back

Part 1 is an exchange. Answer, develop the idea where useful and let the examiner speak. A practice partner can help by changing topics without warning and asking a different question when you expect a familiar one.

If you miss a question, ask for it to be repeated. If the examiner moves on, leave the unfinished detail and listen. You are practising attention as well as English. The ability to respond to a small change in wording is more useful than a polished paragraph that answers a different question.

Questions people ask

How long should an IELTS Speaking Part 1 answer be?

There is no fixed duration or sentence count for each answer. Answer the question directly, then add a relevant reason or detail. Stop when you have explained your meaning instead of repeating the same point.

What topics are in IELTS Speaking Part 1?

Part 1 covers familiar subjects, including home, work, study and everyday interests. Practise explaining your own experiences and preferences, while listening carefully for the particular question and the time it asks about.

What if I have no experience of a Part 1 topic?

Say so briefly and explain the closest relevant experience or preference. If you rarely garden, for example, you can explain why and mention any plants you keep. You do not need to invent an elaborate personal history.
