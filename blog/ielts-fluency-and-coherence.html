IELTS fluency and coherence: keep your answers connected

21 October 2026 · 7 min read

In this guide

  2. Find out why you hesitate

In short

  • Fluency depends on sustained speech and clear development, rather than a target speaking speed.
  • Identify whether a pause comes from missing content or missing language before choosing a drill.
  • Use connectors for a clear purpose and make pronoun references easy for the listener to follow.
  • Keep repairs brief, then check whether the same habit works on an unfamiliar question.

IELTS fluency and coherence is about keeping speech moving while making your ideas easy to follow. Speaking faster does not solve either problem. A quick answer can lose its thread, while a measured answer can develop clearly. Your practice should help you finish thoughts, connect them and recover when a word does not arrive.

The examiner hears this across the Speaking test. Part 1 asks you to respond directly, Part 2 gives you a long turn, and Part 3 asks you to develop broader ideas. You need to adjust the amount of detail to the question rather than use one answer length everywhere.

Keep a useful pace

At a useful pace, you have enough time to form the next phrase without stretching every sentence with empty sounds. There is no target number of words per minute in the public band descriptors. Speed alone does not tell you how fluent an answer is.

Try describing a familiar room. First speak at your normal pace. Then repeat the description with a small pause between complete ideas. Compare how often you restart a sentence and how much effort a listener needs to follow it.

If slowing down makes every word separate, you have gone too far. Keep words together in meaningful groups, such as beside the window or when I get home. Pause between groups where the meaning allows it, without forcing a pause after every group.

Find out why you hesitate

A pause can mean you are deciding what to say. It can also mean you know the idea but cannot find the English. These are different practice problems, even though you may hear the same silence on a recording.

After listening, stop at a long pause and ask yourself what was missing. If you had no example, practise developing reasons. If you could explain the idea in your first language but lacked an English phrase, practise paraphrasing it with words you already know.

The descriptors do not require an absence of hesitation. Band 7 can include some searches for language, repetition or self-correction. The concern is how these affect the flow and the listener's ability to follow you. The guide to the band descriptors explains how this criterion sits alongside the other three.

Give each new sentence a purpose

Coherence depends on the relationship between ideas. Before adding a sentence, consider what it does for the answer. It might explain your reason, give a relevant example or show a limit to your claim. Repeating the claim with different adjectives adds length without development.

Compare this invented answer: I like the park because it's pleasant. It's a nice place and I enjoy it.

A developed version gives the listener something more: I like the park because it's quiet in the morning. I can walk there before work without hearing much traffic, which helps me feel less rushed.

The second answer stays on one reason and explains its effect. Practise adding one useful detail before searching for another reason. In Part 3, that detail can be a plausible general example; you do not need research figures to explain an opinion.

Connect ideas without decorating them

A linking phrase should tell the listener how the next idea relates to the previous one. “For example” introduces an example. “Although” introduces a contrast inside a sentence. Adding either phrase without that relationship makes the answer harder to follow.

Common connectors are useful when they fit. You do not need to replace every “and” or “but”. Extend your range by practising a function, rather than memorising a list of impressive alternatives.

For a qualification, you might say: Working at home saves travel time. That said, it can be difficult to stop thinking about work in the evening.

Read your transcript and circle each connector. Describe its job in a few words. If you cannot identify a job, remove it and say the answer again. Also check that your answer still addresses the question after a contrast or a long example.

Make references easy to follow

Words such as “it”, “they” and “this” connect sentences without repeating a noun every time. They only help when the listener knows what they refer to. This is a quieter part of coherence that a list of linking phrases can miss.

Consider: My cousin spoke to the manager because he was worried. The listener may not know who felt worried. You can remove the doubt: My cousin was worried, so he spoke to the manager.

Listen for “this” at the start of a sentence. If several ideas came before it, name the one you mean: This change in working hours would help parents. Do the same when moving between people in a story. Repeating a noun sometimes improves clarity; avoiding every repetition is not the aim.

Repair an error and keep the sentence moving

Self-correction can make meaning clearer, but repeated repairs can break the thread. During an answer, give priority to errors that change what you mean. If you say “Tuesday” and mean “Thursday”, correct it briefly and continue.

We met on Tuesday, sorry, Thursday, after work.

There is no need to restart the whole answer. If you begin a sentence that becomes too complicated, finish the idea in a shorter form: What I mean is that the cost would be too high. Use that phrase when you need to clarify, rather than adding it to every response.

Save smaller recurring errors for practice afterwards. Repeatedly stopping to search for perfect grammar can make it harder to complete the thought. In your next attempt, work on one correction you have already identified.

Use drills that isolate the difficulty

Choose a drill after listening to an answer. Each of these addresses a different obstacle, so doing all of them every day is unnecessary.

  • For unfinished ideas: answer a familiar question, then explain one reason and its effect. Stop when that thought is complete.
  • For missing words: describe an everyday object without naming it. Explain what it does and where you use it.
  • For weak connections: give an opinion, then add a contrasting case. Make clear when your opinion might not apply.
  • For repeated restarts: speak for 1 minute about a routine. Repair only slips that change the meaning, then review the recording.

After a drill, answer a normal practice question. An isolated exercise is useful only if the habit becomes available during a conversation.

Check progress on a new question

Use a 10-minute session. Spend 2 minutes recording an answer, 3 minutes finding one point where the thread breaks, and 2 minutes trying the answer again. Use the remaining 3 minutes for a different question and a short review.

Keep the comparison specific. Did you complete the reason before starting the example? Did “they” clearly refer to one group? Did a repair stay short enough for the listener to remember the sentence? Those observations are more useful than deciding that you sounded confident.

A smoother second attempt may reflect familiarity with the content. The new question shows whether you can use the same habit without rehearsing its words. Return to the recording a few days later and check if the pattern has changed across several answers.

Questions people ask

Does speaking faster improve IELTS fluency?

Faster speech does not necessarily improve fluency. A useful pace lets you finish thoughts and connect ideas without repeated restarts. The public band descriptors do not set a words-per-minute target. Record yourself and listen for clear development rather than speed.

Can I pause during IELTS Speaking?

Yes. Pauses can help separate ideas, and some hesitation can remain at Band 7. Listen for pauses that repeatedly interrupt meaning or leave sentences unfinished. Work out whether you need more ideas or a way to express an idea with language you already know.

How do I improve coherence in IELTS Speaking?

Develop one point with a reason or relevant example, use linking phrases that match the relationship between ideas, and make references such as “it” and “they” clear. Review a recording, change one weak connection and try the same skill on a new question.
